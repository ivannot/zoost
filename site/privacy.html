Privacy policy

Zoost - Chrome extensions · Last updated: 22 August 2026

The short version

The developer collects nothing. Zoost has no server, no account, no analytics and no tracking of any kind. Your data moves between your browser, the Zoho product you are already signed in to, and a folder on your own disk. Two things leave, and only if you make them. The optional AI assistant sends what you ask it about to the AI provider you configure with your own API key. And Report this problem sends a report you have read in full, to the developer, where it becomes a public issue - that one is described in Where data travels. Nothing else is sent anywhere, and nothing at all is sent without a click of yours.

0. Which extensions this covers

This policy covers every extension in the Zoost family. There are two:

  • Zoost - workbench for Zoho CRM, published on the Chrome Web Store (its listing is being republished this week).
  • Zoost - workbench for Zoho Analytics, published on the Chrome Web Store (its listing is being republished this week).

They are separate extensions with separate permissions, but they behave identically in every respect this document describes: no server, no account, no telemetry, a local mirror in a folder you choose, and one optional AI assistant that only ever talks to a provider you configure yourself.

One difference is worth stating plainly. Zoost CRM can send your Deluge source code to an AI provider if you enable the assistant. Zoost Analytics can send view names, column names, relations and the SQL of your query tables - and never the rows inside your tables, because it does not read them: the endpoints that return cell values are deliberately not called.

1. Who is behind Zoost

Zoost is developed and published by Ivan Notaristefano, an individual developer, as an independent, unofficial tool. Contact: ivan@zoost.it.

For the data Zoost touches, the developer carries out no processing at all: receives none of it, stores none of it, and has no access to the folder the extension writes to. The one thing that can reach the developer is a problem report, which you write, read in full and send by hand; it is set out in §2 and it becomes a public issue, never a record kept here.

On that footing the developer is neither the controller nor a processor of that data. The controller is your own organisation, exactly as it was before Zoost was installed.

This is written down so that nobody assessing the extension has to infer it. It is not legal advice, and every fact it rests on is checkable in the manifest and in the source. The one exception is this website, set out in §8.

Zoost is not affiliated with, endorsed by, sponsored by or supported by Zoho Corporation. Data held inside your Zoho products remains governed by your own agreement with Zoho and by your organisation's policies; this document covers only what the Zoost extensions do with it.

2. What the developer collects

Nothing, unless you send it - and then only what you have read first. The extension contains no analytics, no telemetry, no advertising identifiers, no cookies and no remotely hosted code. Nothing is ever transmitted in the background, on a schedule, or on an error.

There is one channel, and it starts with a click of yours. When something fails, the panel can prepare a problem report and open zoost.it/report in a window of its own, putting the text into the box on that page - through the page's own contents, never through the address, so nothing about it is recorded in your browser's history. Opening that page is an ordinary visit to this website, covered by §8; the report itself is still on your machine, in front of you, and it is sent only when you press send. It then becomes a public issue on the Zoost repository on GitHub, which is permanent. That page can also be used without a panel: somebody who arrives at it from a link writes the problem in their own words and sends that, which is what a reader with no GitHub account has instead of opening an issue - the other being an email, which is not public. It carries no trace, and the issue it opens says so on its first line.

What the report may contain is a fixed list decided in the code, not a sweep of whatever is around: the extension and browser versions, the error and where in Zoost's own files it happened, the last few lines the status bar showed, and counters - how many functions, how many views. It never contains your source code, your SQL, the contents of any file, or your API key: no file of yours is read to build it.

The status lines are the part to understand. They are sentences this panel wrote while you worked, and some of them mention things by name - a folder you chose, a function that synced. Before you see the report, everything that can be recognised as a name, a path, an id, an address or a quoted value is replaced with a placeholder, by the extension and again by the server, and the report says how many replacements were made. What we do not claim is that this is exhaustive: recognising a name is a judgement, and that is exactly why the text is put in front of you before anything happens, and why you can unlock it and cut anything you like - the report then travels marked as altered, so nobody mistakes an edited trace for evidence.

Apart from that, the developer does not sell, share, transfer or otherwise process your personal data. A report you send is published as it stands and joined to nothing, because there is nothing here to join it to.

If you are assessing this: the mirror does contain some personal data about people in your organisation - who last changed a Deluge function, who owns a view - and any personal data somebody typed into the code itself, such as an address in a sendmail. Treat an export the way you would treat the Zoho screens it was read from.

None of this is transmitted anywhere. You can erase all of it at any time by removing the extension and deleting the working folder. Removing the extension deletes the browser-side storage; the local folder is yours and is left untouched.

4. Where data travels

4.1 Your Zoho product

Zoost calls the API of the Zoho product open in your tab - the Zoho CRM API for one extension, the Zoho Analytics API for the other - using the session you are already signed in with. It sends no credentials of its own and creates none. It operates strictly within your existing Zoho permissions: it can read exactly what your Zoho user can read, and nothing more.

Zoost reads metadata and function source. It does not read, download or export your Zoho CRM records - no contacts, no deals, no customer data.

4.2 The AI provider - only if you enable it

Read this before enabling the assistant

The AI assistant is off by default and requires you to enter your own API key. Once enabled, the content it needs to answer you is sent directly from your browser to the provider you selected, Anthropic or OpenAI. For Zoost CRM that content is Deluge source code, module schemas, workflow definitions, schedules, automation actions (their names, kinds, the field an update writes and the value it writes, and a webhook's method, host and path - never its query string, which is where a token usually sits), connection names and their connectors (never their secrets or tokens, which Zoost never reads), what Zoho reports about failed executions - function names, Zoho's own reason text, run counts and credit usage - the name Zoho records against a function or a connection as having last changed it, and your questions. Sender addresses on an email action are held back unless you switch that on in Settings. No other destination is possible: the extension is granted network access to those two origins and to your Zoho product, and to nothing else.

For Zoost Analytics the same applies to a different set: view names, column names and data types, the relations between tables, the dependency graph, and the SQL of your query tables. It can never send the rows inside a table, because it never reads them - so the assistant knows your workspace's structure and never its values.

That provider then processes the data under its own terms and privacy policy, not this one. In that exchange the controller is you, or your organisation, and the AI provider is your supplier, not the developer's. The developer of Zoost is not a party to it, cannot see it and has no control over it. If your organisation restricts sending source code, SQL or business logic to third-party AI services, do not enable this feature.

Your API key is stored on your machine in chrome.storage.local, which is where a Chrome extension keeps its settings, and it is sent only to the provider it belongs to. By default it is stored in clear text: Chrome offers extensions no encryption at rest and no credential store, so anyone with access to your browser profile can read it.

You can turn on passphrase protection in Settings. The key is then encrypted with a passphrase only you know - PBKDF2-SHA256 over 600,000 iterations, then AES-GCM-256, both from the browser's own Web Crypto - and what sits on disk is ciphertext. You type the passphrase once per browser session, in the AI panel; the unlocked key is held in memory (chrome.storage.session) and disappears when the browser closes. The passphrase itself is never stored, never sent anywhere, and cannot be recovered: if you forget it, you enter your API key again and choose a new one.

The switch works in both directions. Turning the protection off asks for the current passphrase, because clear text means decrypting the key first; if it is wrong or missing, nothing is changed and the encrypted key is kept rather than discarded. Losing the passphrase costs you the stored key, never anything else.

It is off by default because the trade is yours to price, not mine. On a personal machine, typing a passphrase each session buys little; on a shared or managed one it buys a great deal.

Note the honest limit of what encryption can do here. While the key is unlocked it is in the browser's memory, where the browser can read it. What the passphrase protects is the key at rest: a copied profile, a backup, another user of the same machine.

4.3 Exports

Export files are written to your working folder and go nowhere on their own. Before each export, Zoost asks which sections may be included; the sensitive part is opt-in and flagged when selected - Deluge source code in Zoost CRM, the SQL of your query tables in Zoost Analytics. What happens to a file after you create it is your responsibility and that of the organisation whose data it describes.

5. Permissions, and why each one exists

Both extensions request the same four API permissions - sidePanel, storage, scripting and tabs - for the same reasons. They differ only in which hosts they may reach: the Zoho CRM one lists the Zoho CRM and sandbox data centres and the Zoho One data centres, the Zoho Analytics one lists the Zoho Analytics data centres. Both list api.anthropic.com and api.openai.com, used only by the optional assistant and only if you configure it.

  • sidePanel - the extension's interface is a Chrome side panel.
  • storage - to keep your settings, as described above.
  • scripting - to inject the bridge script that talks to the Zoho API using your session, and the hook that notices when you save a function so the local copy can follow.
  • tabs - to detect which Zoho CRM org, or which Zoho Analytics workspace, the active tab belongs to, so that a production workspace can never be synced against a sandbox by mistake.
  • Host access to the Zoho domains each extension reads. The Zoho CRM workbench lists crm.zoho.* and crmsandbox.zoho.*, with their crm.zohocloud.ca and crmsandbox.zohocloud.ca counterparts. It also lists one.zoho.* and one.zohocloud.ca, which it does not read: those are there only so the panel can tell that the tab you are on belongs to Zoho One, and which org it is. The Zoho Analytics one lists analytics.zoho.* and analytics.zohocloud.ca.

    Both list nine data centres each, the Saudi, British and Emirati ones included: an account may live on any of them, and an extension that cannot reach yours is inert on it. Zoho runs a tenth in mainland China (zoho.com.cn). Neither extension asks for it - nothing here can test that one, and a host we cannot test is not a host we request.
  • Host access to api.anthropic.com and api.openai.com - the only two AI destinations the extension can reach, used solely by the optional AI assistant and only after you configure it.
  • Host access to zoost.it - this site, and only to fill in the report form. When you press Report this problem the panel opens zoost.it/report in its own window and writes the text into the page, so you read it in full before anything is sent. Nothing is read back from this site: the extensions never fetch it, and the only requests to it are the pages your browser opens when you click that button or one of the links in About.

Zoost requests no access to your browsing history, bookmarks, downloads, saved passwords or any site other than those listed above.

Both extensions also declare a content security policy of their own: script-src 'self'; object-src 'self'; base-uri 'self'; form-action 'none'. The first two are what Manifest V3 already enforces - no inline script, no code loaded from anywhere else - written down rather than relied on silently. The last two are stricter than the default and cost nothing, because nothing shipped uses a form or a <base> element. A policy can only be tightened this way: Chrome refuses a manifest that tries to relax the first two.

6. Legal basis and your rights

The developer receives nothing except a problem report you chose to send, and that is published rather than held, so there is no store to consent to, object to, or request access to. A report is a public issue: ask and it is deleted. Data protection rights in respect of the Zoho data itself are exercised against the controller of that data - your organisation - and, where relevant, against the AI provider you chose, under their respective policies.

7. Children

Zoost is a developer and administration tool for business software. It is not directed at children and is not intended for use by anyone under 16.

8. This website

zoost.it sets no cookies, embeds no trackers, loads no third-party fonts or analytics, and does not profile visitors. The pages are static files, and every page but one runs a single script served from this same domain, which fills in the copyright year and the version badge in the footer.

The one exception is the problem-report page, which also loads Cloudflare's Turnstile from challenges.cloudflare.com. It is the anti-abuse check standing between that form and a public issue tracker, and it is the only third-party script on this site. It runs on that page and nowhere else. Cloudflare is given your address for the check, which is described below - it is already the host of this site, so it has it either way.

The badge calls one endpoint on this site, /api/versions. It runs on Cloudflare's edge and answers from two things: a reading of the Chrome Web Store, taken by a scheduled job in the public repository and stored at the edge rather than fetched while you wait, and GitHub for the repository's newest tag, the version in its manifest and the date the site content last changed. That is how the footer can show what is published, what was released, what is in development, and when these pages last changed.

Those requests are made by the server, not by your browser. Nothing about you is sent to Google or GitHub, no identifier of yours is involved, and the answer is cached for everyone alike. If a source is unreachable the badge simply reads "unknown".

What sending a problem report leaves behind. The report itself is published as a public issue and is described in section 2. Two things about the request are worth stating here, because they concern you rather than the report: Cloudflare's Turnstile is given your IP address to run its check, and the endpoint keeps a counter so that one address does not open more than five issues a day. That counter is a damper and not a lock: it is stored in an eventually consistent store and read through a cache, so several reports sent in the same few seconds can pass together. What actually bounds a burst is the anti-abuse check in front of it. That counter is stored against a salted hash of the address and expires after 24 hours: your address is not written down, and what is written cannot be turned back into it - not even by whoever reads the store. Nothing else about the request is kept, and none of it is joined to the report.

The one place where the developer is the controller. This site is served by Cloudflare, which is both its network and its host. Like any web server, its edge keeps ordinary request logs - IP address, time, URL, user agent - and counts requests without setting a cookie or an identifier. That is a processing of personal data, however slight, and for it the controller is the developer, on the legitimate interest in serving and protecting the site. Nothing is built on top of it: no cookie, no tracker, no profile, no mailing list, and nothing here is joined to anything the extension does. Cloudflare's own retention applies; no log export is enabled and the developer keeps no copy of any of it. To exercise a right over that, or to ask anything about it: ivan@zoost.it.
